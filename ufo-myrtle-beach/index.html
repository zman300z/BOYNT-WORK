Unidentified craft comes down in the Atlantic four miles off Myrtle Beach

Radar tracked the object on a descending path out of low Earth orbit before it entered the water shortly after 3 a.m. Officials say it matches nothing in the public catalog of satellites, spent boosters or debris — and no country has claimed it.

MR By Marisol Reyes and Dean Ottaway Tidewater Signal · Coastal Bureau Published Aug. 19, 2026, 5:12 a.m. ET Updated 8:47 a.m. ET

MYRTLE BEACH, S.C. — Something came down out of the sky over the Grand Strand early Wednesday morning, and more than five hours later nobody in a position to know would say what it was.

The object entered the Atlantic roughly four nautical miles east-southeast of the 14th Avenue Pier at 3:07 a.m., according to two people briefed on the tracking data who spoke on condition of anonymity because they were not authorized to discuss it. It did not sink. As of 8:30 a.m. it was still there, sitting low and mostly submerged, ringed by three Coast Guard cutters and a two-mile exclusion zone that the Coast Guard's Sector Charleston office extended twice before sunrise.

What has federal agencies moving quickly is not the splashdown itself — the ocean off the Carolinas catches falling hardware a few times a year — but the path it took getting here.

Radar returns reviewed by the Signal show the object in a stable, roughly circular orbit about 210 miles up for at least four passes before it began to descend. Then, over the course of a single 90-minute orbit, it dropped. The descent was smooth and shallow, and it slowed at the end.

It came out of orbit. That part is not in dispute and it is not interesting. What is interesting is that it was in orbit at all, and we cannot tell you when it got there. — A senior official at a U.S. space-tracking facility, speaking on condition of anonymity

Every object larger than about four inches in Earth orbit is supposed to be in a catalog. There are more than 40,000 of them: working satellites, dead satellites, spent upper stages, bolts, paint flecks, a lost tool bag. Each is tracked, numbered and matched to a launch. Objects do not simply appear in the catalog. They are put into orbit by rockets, and rockets are visible from a long way off.

This one is not in the catalog. Three current and former tracking officials confirmed that no entry in the public catalog matches its orbit, and none of them could point to a launch it could plausibly have come from.

Where it came from

The honest answer, five people involved in the response said in some version or another, is that nobody knows.

The object was first noted at 11:52 p.m. Tuesday, already in orbit, on a track that crossed the eastern seaboard on a northwest-to-southeast diagonal. Analysts initially logged it as an uncorrelated target — routine, usually resolved within a day when the object turns out to be a fragment of something already known. It was not resolved. By 1:30 a.m. the track had been elevated.

Working backward from the descent, the object's earlier orbit does not extrapolate cleanly to any launch window in the last six weeks. That leaves two possibilities that space-surveillance people are willing to say out loud: either it was launched by someone who did not want it seen and it went unnoticed for some period, or it arrived from outside Earth orbit and inserted itself — which is a very different sentence, and one that no official would put their name to on Wednesday morning.

What we know, what we don't

Known: An object approximately 55–70 feet across entered the water 4.1 nautical miles off Myrtle Beach at 3:07 a.m. It descended from a low Earth orbit at roughly 210 miles. It is intact, floating and emitting light on an irregular cycle. Federal agencies have closed the airspace and the water around it.

Not known: What it is, who built it, how long it was in orbit, how it got into orbit, whether anything aboard is alive, and whether the light is a signal, a byproduct or an accident.

On the beach

The first calls to Horry County dispatch came in at 3:04 a.m., three minutes before the water. Callers described a light "like a welding arc" moving south to north, slower than a meteor and without the sound that usually follows one.

Delia Karounos, 62, was walking her dog on the beach near 29th Avenue North when she saw it. "I've lived here forty years and I've watched the rockets go up from the Cape and come down again," she said. "That's a rocket. It roars, it's over, it's gone. This one took its time. It went out over the water and it stopped. Things do not stop."

Nolan Vestrick, a charter captain who had just cast off from the marina at Little River, said he was inside the exclusion zone when it was established. "The water lit up under the boat before I saw anything on the surface," he said. "Green, then orange. My depth sounder went to garbage for about a minute — just noise. Then it came back and there it was, two hundred yards off my starboard, half under."

The Coast Guard ordered him out at 3:40 a.m. He said cutters were already on station when he turned for shore, which would put them on the water before the object came down.

The trajectory

The Signal reconstructed the descent from radar returns and two independent amateur-satellite observers in Georgia and Virginia who recorded the object on its final two passes. The reconstruction is approximate, but the shape of it is not in question: a stable orbit, a controlled-looking drop, and a terminal deceleration that a ballistic object cannot perform.

Dr. Priya Anand, an orbital-dynamics researcher at a Southeastern university who reviewed the Signal's reconstruction, cautioned that amateur observations carry wide error bars. She would not endorse the deceleration figure. But she said the descent profile itself was, in her word, "wrong."

Things that fall out of orbit fall on a curve you can write down in one line. This did not follow that curve. Either the observations are bad, or the object was doing something. — Dr. Priya Anand, orbital dynamics

The response

By 4:15 a.m. the Federal Aviation Administration had issued a temporary flight restriction covering a ten-nautical-mile radius from the surface to 18,000 feet. The Coast Guard's exclusion zone went to two nautical miles at 5:30 a.m. Horry County closed beach access from 21st Avenue North to 38th Avenue North "pending a federal operation," a phrase the county could not expand on when asked.

A Coast Guard spokesperson confirmed the exclusion zone and the presence of three cutters and referred all questions about the object to "federal partners," which she declined to name. The FAA confirmed the flight restriction and said its origin was "an interagency request." The Department of Defense did not respond to four messages. NASA said the object is not theirs and that they have no launches in that inclination.

Two Navy aircraft flew racetrack patterns over the site through the morning. A salvage-capable vessel left Charleston at 6:20 a.m. on a heading that would put it on scene before noon, according to public vessel-tracking data reviewed by the Signal.

Nothing has been recovered. Nothing has been named. At 8:40 a.m., from the boardwalk, the exclusion zone was a line of gray hulls on a flat sea, and past them a shape in the water that the light kept finding and losing.

This is a developing story and will be updated. Marisol Reyes reported from Myrtle Beach and Dean Ottaway from Columbia. Reporting contributed by the Signal's Charleston bureau.
