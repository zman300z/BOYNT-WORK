A closed beach in the busiest week of the year: the Grand Strand is living underneath this

Fourteen blocks of sand are off limits. The charter fleet is tied to the dock. City Hall says federal agencies have told it almost nothing. And every twenty minutes or so, everybody on the Strand looks up.

DW By Dara Whitlock and Cam Rutherford · MBC News Published Aug. 19, 2026, 12:15 p.m. Updated 1:04 p.m.

The beach at 27th Avenue North, closed since before dawn on what would normally be one of the ten busiest days of the year. Horry County Beach Patrol has been turning people back at every access point between 21st and 38th. MBC 12 / Cam Rutherford

MYRTLE BEACH — Whatever is sitting in the water four miles out, the Grand Strand is the place that has to live next to it.

By late Wednesday morning that meant fourteen closed blocks of the most valuable sand in South Carolina, a fishing fleet stuck at the dock on a flat-calm day, a boardwalk packed with people who came to look at a horizon where there is not much to see, and a city government that has spent nine hours asking federal agencies the same question and getting the same non-answer.

"I have three federal phone numbers and none of them will tell me what my own beach is closed for," said Renny Alcott, a Myrtle Beach city councilman whose district includes the closed stretch. "I'm not asking them to tell me what it is. I'm asking them how long."

Fourteen blocks, in the third week of August

The closure runs from 21st Avenue North to 38th Avenue North — public access points, the hotel walkovers, and the beach itself. Horry County Beach Patrol has staffed every entrance since 5 a.m. The rule they are enforcing came down from the county, which got it from the Coast Guard, which has not explained it to them either.

"People are being good about it, mostly," said Marla DeSoto, a beach patrol supervisor working the line at 27th. "But I've turned back probably four hundred people this morning and I've told every single one of them the same thing, which is that I don't know. That gets old for them and it gets old for me."

Third week of August is not a small thing to lose here. The stretch inside the closure holds roughly 2,900 hotel rooms. The businesses on Ocean Boulevard behind it — the pancake houses, the surf shops, the arcade at 29th — depend on a foot traffic that on Wednesday was pointed the wrong way, north toward the barricades instead of down onto the sand.

The sound every twenty minutes

The thing nobody on the Strand can ignore is not visible. It is audible.

Military aircraft have been running passes over the site since 3:19 a.m., and the low ones cross the beachfront on their way in. MBC 12 counted eleven passes audible from our Ocean Boulevard studio between 6 a.m. and noon. Two of them shook the glass.

Above those, according to national reporting and confirmed to MBC by a Northern Command spokesperson, a pair of fighter jets has held a continuous orbit over the site since before dawn — refueled in the air to keep them up there. Officials have been careful to describe those jets as standing by to assist rather than as an intercept posture, and locals have received that message in two very different ways.

I'm a Navy wife for twenty-six years and I know exactly what I'm hearing up there. It doesn't scare me. What would scare me is if they left. Jeanette Prue, Socastee

Others put it the other way around. "You don't put jets over a beach in August because everything's fine," said Owen Marchetti, who drove down from Conway with his son to stand at the fence at 33rd. "That's not a criticism. I just don't think anyone should pretend that's normal."

The passes have also produced a measurable municipal problem. Horry County's non-emergency line took more than 600 calls between midnight and noon, and emergency management asked the public twice to stop calling 911 to report aircraft noise or lights. The call pattern tracks the flights almost exactly.

Tied up at the dock

At the marinas, Wednesday was a total loss and everybody knew it by 4 a.m.

The exclusion zone sits directly across the run that the half-day and full-day boats take out of Little River and Murrells Inlet. Three marinas cancelled every departure. Captains estimated the lost revenue at $1,800 to $4,000 per boat for the day, on a Wednesday in August that was booked solid weeks ago.

"Flat seas, no wind, eighty-four degrees, and I'm standing on a dock," said Nolan Vestrick, a charter captain who was on the water when the object came down and was ordered out of the zone at 3:40 a.m. "I've been running this coast for nineteen years and I've never once been shut down by anything but weather."

Vestrick said he has been fielding calls all morning from customers who want to rebook, and a different set of calls from people who want to charter a boat to go look at it. He has turned those down. "It's a two-mile ring with federal boats in it. There's no version of that where I take paying people out there."

Cancellations, then a rush

Hotels along the closed stretch reported a wave of cancellations that started around 6 a.m. and largely stopped by 9 — and then reversed.

Two property managers told MBC 12 that by late morning they were booking more rooms than they had lost, from people driving in specifically because of the story. One reported forty-one new reservations before noon, most of them for tonight.

On Ocean Boulevard, a shop at 26th had shirts in the window by 9:30 reading I WAS IN MYRTLE BEACH AUGUST 19, 2026. The owner said he printed the first two dozen at 7 a.m. and had sold out of them by 11.

The city's tourism office declined to characterize the net effect, saying it was "not the day for that arithmetic."

What the city can — and can't — tell you

The most consistent complaint on the Grand Strand Wednesday was not about the closure. It was about the silence behind it.

Myrtle Beach's emergency manager has been on three interagency calls since 5 a.m. and told MBC 12 that the city has received no substantive briefing on what the object is, how long the closure will last, or what would trigger a wider evacuation. City officials were told there is no evacuation planned and no shelter-in-place order contemplated — and were given nothing further.

Rumors have filled that space quickly. By mid-morning the city was publicly knocking down three of them: there is no order to evacuate, the municipal water supply is unaffected, and the beach south of 21st is open and safe.

The federal government is doing its job out there and I'm not going to second-guess it. But somebody needs to understand that a closed beach in August is not an abstraction here. It's payroll. Renny Alcott, Myrtle Beach city council

The county says it will reassess the closure at 6 p.m. Whether that means it lifts, holds, or grows is — like nearly everything else about Wednesday — not something anyone here has been told.
