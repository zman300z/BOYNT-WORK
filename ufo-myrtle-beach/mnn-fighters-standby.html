Fighter jets standing by off the South Carolina coast as military aircraft run repeated passes over the object

At least fourteen overflights since before dawn. Officials say the jets are overhead to observe and to be close enough to help if anyone needs it — not to intercept anything.

What to know

  • Military aircraft have flown at least 14 passes over the object since 3:19 a.m., the first twelve minutes after it entered the water.
  • A two-ship of fighters has held a continuous orbit above the site, refueled in the air to stay on station.
  • Officials describe the fighters as standing by to assist — not as an intercept posture. No weapons employment has been authorized or requested.
  • A rescue helicopter and swimmer team are on alert in case anyone on the water gets into trouble.
  • The airspace is closed within 10 nautical miles to 18,000 feet. The water is closed within two.

MYRTLE BEACH, S.C. — For nine hours, the loudest thing on the Grand Strand has been the sound of American fighter jets going around again.

Military aircraft have made at least fourteen passes over the unidentified object sitting in the water four miles off this city since it came down at 3:07 a.m. Wednesday, according to a Meridian News Network count corroborated by two defense officials. The first came at 3:19 a.m. The most recent was at 11:48 a.m., low and out of the north, and it rattled the windows of the beachfront hotels it crossed.

Above those passes, higher and slower, two fighters have held a continuous orbit since before dawn. They have been relieved twice and refueled in the air at least three times to keep the station unbroken. Officials have gone out of their way — repeatedly, and unprompted — to explain what those jets are and are not there to do.

They are standing by. That is the whole description. If a boat gets in trouble inside that zone, if an aircraft strays in, if something down there changes and somebody needs help fast, I want airplanes already overhead and not sitting on a ramp forty minutes away. Col. Wade Ferrante, Air Component Coordination Element

Asked directly whether the fighters are armed, Ferrante said the aircraft are "configured as they normally launch," and declined to go further. Asked whether any authority to engage the object had been sought or granted, he said no — twice, and without qualification.

What the passes are for

The overflights are not the fighters. They are being flown by a mix of aircraft — a maritime patrol plane working a wide search pattern, a helicopter carrying survey equipment, and at least two passes by a reconnaissance aircraft — and their purpose is to build a picture of something no one has been able to identify from a distance.

Each pass collects a different slice: optical and infrared imagery, radar returns from a low angle, air sampling downwind of the object, and radiation readings. A defense official said the survey aircraft has found "nothing above background" — no elevated radiation and no chemical signature in the air over the site — a finding first reported by the Tidewater Signal and since confirmed to MNN by a second official.

The object has done nothing in response. It has not moved, has not transmitted on any monitored frequency, and has not reacted to the aircraft crossing above it — including the low passes, which two officials confirmed were flown in part to see whether it would.

Standing by, and what that means

"Standing by" is not a figure of speech in this context. It is a posture with a definition, and three officials walked MNN through it the same way.

Aircraft on station overhead are the fastest possible response to anything that happens below them, and the reasons they might be needed are mostly mundane: a recreational boat that pushes the exclusion line and swamps, a drone operator whose aircraft goes into the water near a cutter, a crewman hurt on a small boat working the perimeter. A fighter cannot pull anyone out of the water — but it can find them in seconds, mark the position, and hold overhead until a helicopter arrives.

That helicopter is also standing by. A rescue crew with swimmers has been on alert at a coastal air station since 4 a.m., and a second aircrew went to alert status at 9. Neither has launched.

The less mundane version is the one nobody will discuss in specifics. Pressed on what else the fighters would be reacting to, Ferrante said only that "an unidentified object is unidentified in every direction" — including, he allowed, the possibility that it does something no one has planned for.

On the ground, a city listening

The effect on shore has been less about what people can see than what they can hear. The object itself is a smudge on the horizon, visible with binoculars and not much else. The aircraft are unmistakable.

"You feel the low ones in the balcony rail," said Priscilla Vance, who has been on the fourteenth floor of a hotel at 32nd Avenue North since Monday and has not left it since 4 a.m. "It's every twenty minutes or so. My grandkids think it's the best week of their lives. I've counted eleven and I've been asleep for two of them."

Horry County has kept the beach closed from 21st to 38th Avenue North, and the sand outside that line has filled with people watching a sky that mostly looks empty until it very suddenly doesn't. Emergency management asked residents twice Wednesday morning to stop calling 911 to report the noise.

Hotels along the closed stretch reported a run of cancellations before 8 a.m. and a heavier run of new bookings after 10.

What officials still won't say

Nine hours in, the list of unanswered questions has not shortened. No agency has claimed the object. No government has said it launched anything matching it. The military will not characterize it, will not say whether it is considered a threat, and will not describe what the survey passes have actually returned beyond the radiation and chemical readings.

Ferrante was asked, at the end of a fifteen-minute call, the simplest version of the question: is the public in danger?

"Right now, from that object, we have no indication that anybody is," he said. "That is a real answer and it is not a promise. It's why the airplanes are up."

Partner coverage The Tidewater Signal is reporting on the ground in Myrtle Beach and first published the radar reconstruction showing the object's descent out of orbit — and the fact that it matches nothing in the public catalog of more than 40,000 tracked objects. MBC 12 is covering what the closure and the overflights are doing to the Grand Strand itself.
