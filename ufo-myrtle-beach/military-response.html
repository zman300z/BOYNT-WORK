Fighters, a destroyer and a ten-mile no-fly ring: the military takes the lead off Myrtle Beach

Two F-16s were over the site within twelve minutes of the splashdown. By mid-morning the response had grown to a destroyer, a maritime patrol aircraft, a radiological survey helicopter and a dive team. Officials call the posture precautionary — and will not say what they are guarding against.

CV By Curtis Vandermeer and Marisol Reyes Tidewater Signal · Defense & Coastal Bureaus Published Aug. 19, 2026, 9:05 a.m. ET Updated 11:52 a.m. ET

MYRTLE BEACH, S.C. — Twelve minutes after the object hit the water, two fighters were on top of it.

They came from the west out of a South Carolina Air National Guard base, already airborne on an unrelated night training sortie when the tasking came, according to a Northern Command spokesperson, Maj. Elise Trant. The first pass was at 3:19 a.m., high and fast. The second, eleven minutes later, was low enough that people on the boardwalk heard it before they saw it.

What the pilots reported has not been released. What has been released is the shape of the response since, and it has only grown: a continuous fighter orbit over the site, a maritime patrol aircraft flying a search pattern out to twenty miles, a helicopter carrying radiological and chemical survey equipment, an Arleigh Burke-class destroyer that arrived shortly before 10 a.m., and an explosive ordnance disposal dive team staged aboard it.

At 6 a.m., U.S. Northern Command assumed lead of the response from the Coast Guard. The Coast Guard still owns the water. The military owns the question.

Nobody is going to shoot at it. That is not what this is. We put airplanes over an unidentified object in American water because that is the job, and because until somebody can tell me what it is, the safe assumption is that I don't know. — Maj. Elise Trant, U.S. Northern Command

What the fighters are actually doing

Very little, deliberately. Three defense officials described the fighter orbit the same way: eyes, and a clock.

The aircraft are flying a racetrack pattern northeast of the site at what one official called "a comfortable altitude" — high enough that a surprise from below would not put them inside it. Their job is to watch the object, to keep civil aircraft out of a closed piece of sky, and to be able to react in minutes rather than in the forty it would take to launch from a cold start.

They are not, officials stressed repeatedly, an attack posture. Under standing rules, an intercept requires a hostile act or demonstrated hostile intent, and the object has done neither. It has floated. It has glowed on a cycle. It has not moved, transmitted on any monitored band, or responded to anything directed at it — including, according to one official, a series of directed light signals attempted from a cutter around 5 a.m.

Why close the airspace at all?

Debris and fuel. Anything that comes out of orbit intact may still be carrying pressurized tanks, hypergolic propellant or a power source. Standard practice around a downed spacecraft is a wide keep-out radius until a survey team clears it — the same procedure used for a failed booster stage.

Air traffic. A ten-mile ring to 18,000 feet keeps sightseers, news helicopters and drones out of the way of the aircraft actually working the site. Two civilian drones were recovered from the beach by 8 a.m., and the FAA warned that operators face penalties.

The unknown part. Officials would not describe what other hazard they are accounting for, other than to say the survey helicopter's readings were "nothing above background" — no elevated radiation, no chemical signature in the air over the site.

The safety case, and its limits

Every official who spoke Wednesday returned to the same word: precautionary. The exclusion zone is precautionary. The flight restriction is precautionary. The destroyer is precautionary.

Dr. Alan Ruiz-Beck, a former range safety officer who now consults on launch and recovery operations, said the posture is exactly what he would expect for a piece of hardware of unknown provenance sitting in shallow water four miles from a city of 40,000 that swells past 400,000 in August.

"You do not need to believe anything exotic to justify any of this," he said. "You need to believe there is a large object made of unknown materials in the water near a beach with a hundred thousand people on it. That is enough. That was always enough."

The limit of the safety framing is what it does not cover. A destroyer is not required to keep swimmers away from debris. Neither is a fighter orbit. Asked directly whether the military considers the object a potential threat, Trant said she would not characterize it, and then added a sentence that the Signal has confirmed with a second official: the response is being run under a contingency framework normally used for a foreign object recovered on American soil.

The closed sky, mapped

The temporary flight restriction issued at 4:15 a.m. covers a ten-nautical-mile circle from the surface to 18,000 feet, which takes in the entire Grand Strand shoreline and both airports serving it. Commercial traffic into Myrtle Beach International is being vectored around the northern edge of the ring; eleven arrivals were delayed and two diverted to Wilmington before 9 a.m.

Below, the part nobody is talking about

The dive team is the detail that has drawn the most attention from the people who follow this kind of operation for a living. Explosive ordnance disposal divers are how the Navy approaches an object it does not want to touch with a crane.

Two officials confirmed a team is staged aboard the destroyer. Neither would say whether anyone has been in the water. A third, asked whether a recovery attempt was planned for Wednesday, said only: "Not today."

At the boardwalk railing, that answer landed about as well as it reads. A crowd of several hundred has held the fence line since dawn, watching a gray horizon and, every eleven minutes or so, two dark shapes coming around again.
